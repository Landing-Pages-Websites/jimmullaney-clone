## What Is a Collaborative Divorce in Florida?

A collaborative divorce is a structured, voluntary way for spouses to resolve divorce-related issues outside the traditional courtroom process. Under Florida's Collaborative Law Process Act, the process is intended to resolve qualifying family-law matters without court intervention. Court involvement may still be needed to approve or formalize the result. The process begins with a written participation agreement and follows legal and professional rules for collaborative practice.

Florida's framework generally applies to matters under Chapters 61 and 742, including divorce and paternity actions. It reflects Florida's public policy encouraging the peaceful resolution of disputes and the early resolution of pending litigation. You can review the statutory framework in [Florida Statutes sections 61.55 through 61.57](https://www.leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute\&URL=0000-0099/0061/Sections/0061.55.html).

### What the statutory process involves

Collaborative divorce is more than a private promise to be reasonable. Each spouse normally participates with a collaborative lawyer, and both spouses agree in writing to use the collaborative process to work toward a resolution. The Florida Courts provide a formal Collaborative Law Participation Agreement, identified as Florida Supreme Court Approved Family Law Form 12.985(b)(2). That document helps define the process and the participants' commitments before substantive negotiations begin.

The matters addressed can include the legal and practical issues that must be resolved in a divorce. Such as division of property and debts, spousal support, child support, a parenting plan, and time-sharing. The precise issues depend on the family's circumstances and the agreements the spouses are able to reach. Collaborative professionals may also assist with financial information or family-related concerns when those resources are appropriate.

### How it differs from an informal agreement

Spouses may agree informally about some parts of their separation, but an informal agreement is not automatically a complete or enforceable divorce settlement. A collaborative process uses a defined legal framework, written participation terms, professional guidance, and clear settlement documents. It is designed to address the whole family-law matter carefully rather than leave important questions unresolved.

Participation remains voluntary. Collaborative law does not require either spouse to accept a proposal or continue negotiating indefinitely. If the process does not provide a workable path forward, the spouses may need to consider another dispute-resolution method or litigation. ## How Does the Florida Collaborative Divorce Process Begin?

Beginning a collaborative divorce is less about rushing to court and more about deciding whether both spouses can participate voluntarily in a structured settlement process. Florida's collaborative framework is connected to the Florida Collaborative Law Process Act and related rules of procedure and professional conduct. Florida Statutes section 61.57 addresses the collaborative law process and its effect when the process ends. Because the legal details can affect your options, review the statute with a Florida family law attorney rather than treating this overview as a checklist.

A practical decision sequence may look like this:

1. **Consider whether collaboration is appropriate.** Each spouse should have a meaningful choice about participating. Collaborative divorce is not designed to force someone into negotiations where there is coercion, intimidation, or an inability to participate safely and openly. If voluntary participation is not realistic, another legal process may be more appropriate.
2. **Consult with separate collaborative attorneys.** Each spouse works with their own attorney. Those attorneys advise their respective clients, explain the legal issues, and help them evaluate proposed solutions. One attorney should not act as both spouses' individual advocate in the same matter.
3. **Review and sign a participation agreement.** The process uses a formal Collaborative Law Participation Agreement. Florida Courts identifies this as Florida Supreme Court Approved Family Law Form 12.985(b)(2), an approved family-law form. The agreement establishes that the spouses are choosing the collaborative process and identifies the commitments that govern their work together. Read it carefully before signing. [You can review the Florida Courts participation agreement form here](https://flcourts-media.flcourts.gov/content/download/691100/file_pdf/985b2-10-20.pdf).
4. **Identify the issues that need resolution.** The attorneys and participants can then organize the topics requiring decisions, such as property, support, and parenting arrangements. The process is intended to support peaceful dispute resolution and the early resolution of pending litigation, but it does not guarantee agreement on every issue.
5. **Communicate and reassess as needed.** Participation remains voluntary. If a spouse withdraws or the collaborative process terminates under the applicable agreement and law, the matter may need to proceed through another process. Florida Statutes section 61.57 is an important authority for understanding that transition. Ending collaboration is not a prediction about the final result. It means the parties need legal guidance about their next available step.

## Who Is on a Collaborative Divorce Team?

A collaborative divorce team is built around the two spouses and the professionals who help them make informed decisions. Each spouse has a separate collaborative attorney. Those attorneys advise their own clients, explain legal options, and help protect each client's interests while working toward terms both spouses can accept.

### Each spouse has an independent attorney

The attorneys are not interchangeable. Your attorney is your advocate and legal advisor. The other spouse's attorney serves the other spouse in the same capacity. Having separate counsel gives each person a place to ask candid questions, understand tradeoffs, and review proposed agreements before signing.

This distinction also protects the integrity of the process. One attorney should not represent both spouses in the same divorce. An attorney should not act as one spouse's advocate and the neutral mediator for that same matter. Those roles carry different responsibilities and can create a conflict if combined.

### Other professionals may join when they add value

Some collaborative matters use a broader professional team, while others may need only the spouses and their attorneys. The right structure depends on the issues, the family's circumstances, and what information is needed to reach clear decisions. There is no requirement that every case use the same combination of professionals.

A financial professional may help organize financial information and explain the practical effects of different options. That can be useful when the spouses need to evaluate income, expenses, assets, debts, or support-related choices. The financial professional is not a substitute for either spouse's legal advice. Each attorney still explains how a proposed term fits within the client's legal position.

A mental-health professional may serve as a communication or relationship-focused professional. This person can help spouses communicate more productively, identify concerns that are interfering with decisions, and keep discussions focused on workable solutions. That role is different from providing legal advice or deciding the outcome for the couple.

### How the team differs from mediation

In mediation, a neutral facilitator helps both spouses communicate and explore settlement options. The mediator does not represent either spouse. Each person may consult a separate attorney before, during, or after mediation, but the mediator's role remains neutral.

That is different from collaborative law, where both spouses have attorneys actively participating as their advocates in a structured settlement process. ## How Are Finances, Parenting, and Settlement Terms Resolved?

A collaborative divorce is only as sound as the information and language behind the final agreement. The goal is not simply to reach a verbal understanding. Each spouse needs enough financial information to evaluate options, and the written settlement must address the issues that matter to the family with practical, specific terms.

### Complete financial information comes first

Property and debt decisions require a clear picture of the marital finances. That means identifying the assets, accounts, income, and obligations that may affect settlement discussions. When information is incomplete, a proposal may look workable while leaving important questions unanswered. In a collaborative process, the participants can organize the relevant information, identify disputed issues, and discuss possible resolutions without treating every disagreement as a reason to escalate.

Florida policy encourages the peaceful resolution of disputes and the early resolution of pending litigation. Those principles support a careful, informed settlement process, but they do not eliminate the need for legal analysis. Questions about what is marital or nonmarital, how debts should be addressed, or how property may be divided can have lasting consequences. Learn more about [equitable distribution of property and debts](https://www.jimmullaney.com/divorce/equitable-distribution/) before accepting language that does not reflect your circumstances.

### Support and parenting terms should fit real life

Settlement discussions may also address support and the financial responsibilities connected with raising children. A parenting arrangement should do more than state that both parents will remain involved. Florida uses the term *time-sharing* to describe the schedule and practical arrangements for a child's time with each parent. A parenting plan can address regular schedules, exchanges, holidays, communication, and other details appropriate to the family's situation. Review information about [Florida parenting plans and time-sharing](https://www.jimmullaney.com/time-sharing-and-visitation-in-florida/) for the terminology used in Florida family law.

Specificity matters because vague promises can create fresh disagreement later. The right level of detail depends on the children's needs, the parents' schedules, distance between homes, and other facts. No single schedule or provision is appropriate for every family.

### Final documents must say what the parties actually agreed to

A settlement is not complete merely because the spouses feel that they are generally on the same page. The final documents should accurately capture the decisions on property, debts, support, parenting, and time-sharing. Clear language can reduce uncertainty about responsibilities and make it easier to identify what happens next. A [Florida marital settlement agreement](https://www.jimmullaney.com/divorce/uncontested-divorce-in-jacksonville-fl/florida-marital-settlement-agreements/) is one example of the kind of formal document used to record divorce terms.

Collaborative discussions can help spouses build an agreement, but participation remains voluntary. If material information is not available or important issues cannot be resolved, the family may need to consider another legal process. ## What Happens in Meetings and If the Process Ends?

Collaborative meetings are working sessions, not informal conversations. The purpose is to identify the issues that must be resolved. Exchange the information needed to discuss those issues, and develop terms that both spouses can understand and accept. The process is designed to support a respectful working relationship while the legal and practical details of separation are addressed.

### Prepare to make each meeting useful

Before a meeting, gather the documents and questions that relate to the topics on the agenda. Depending on your circumstances, that may include income information, account statements, debt records, property details, and facts relevant to parenting. Preparation helps the participants focus on decisions instead of trying to reconstruct basic information in the room.

It is also helpful to identify what you need to understand before agreeing to a term. Ask for an explanation when legal language, financial calculations, or parenting provisions are unclear. Each spouse has a collaborative attorney to provide individual legal advice. Other professionals may help organize financial information or address family dynamics, but their roles should be clear. The goal is informed decision-making, not pressure to accept a proposal.

### Turn discussions into clear agreements

Progress should be recorded as the participants reach decisions. A complete settlement document should address the issues that apply to the family, rather than relying on verbal understandings or assumptions. Property and debts should be identified with enough detail to reduce later confusion. Support terms should state the relevant obligations. If children are involved, the parenting plan should explain parental responsibility, time-sharing, communication, transportation, holidays, and other details that matter in daily life.

Once the terms are reviewed, the final documents can be prepared for signing and use in the related family-law matter. You can learn more about the role of a [Florida marital settlement agreement](https://www.jimmullaney.com/divorce/uncontested-divorce-in-jacksonville-fl/florida-marital-settlement-agreements/) before discussing document language with your attorney. Florida's collaborative framework includes rules of procedure and professional conduct, and the Florida Courts provide an approved Collaborative Law Participation Agreement, Form 12.985(b)(2), for this process.

### If someone ends the process

Participation is voluntary. If one spouse decides not to continue, or the participants cannot reach terms through the collaborative process, the matter does not become an agreement simply because meetings occurred. The attorneys should explain the procedural consequences and the available next steps. Those steps may include another settlement process or litigation, depending on the issues and the family's circumstances.

A related court proceeding can also change whether collaborative work remains appropriate. Because the consequences depend on the documents signed, the stage of the matter. And the reason the process ended, obtain individual legal advice before ending participation or filing elsewhere. The practical priority is to document what has been decided, identify what remains unresolved, and protect each person's ability to make an informed choice about the path forward.

## Is Collaborative Divorce Right for Your Jacksonville Family?

For a Jacksonville couple, collaborative divorce may be a useful fit when both spouses voluntarily want to negotiate. Exchange information, and work toward a written resolution outside an adversarial courtroom process. Florida recognizes collaborative law as a family-law dispute-resolution method, and the process begins with a formal participation agreement rather than an informal promise to cooperate. The agreement helps define the process and the participants' commitments.

Cooperation does not mean that you must agree on every issue at the outset. It does mean that each spouse must be able to participate freely and negotiate with meaningful information. If one person is afraid to speak openly, is being pressured to sign, or cannot make decisions independently, collaborative work may not provide a safe or reliable setting. Concerns about coercive control, domestic abuse, or other safety risks should be discussed privately with a qualified attorney before choosing a process.

The same caution applies when financial information is incomplete. Undisclosed assets, unexplained debt, or a refusal to provide needed records can prevent informed decisions. If either spouse will not negotiate in good faith, or if important issues remain impossible to resolve, another path may be more appropriate. Florida's framework includes a formal participation agreement and rules governing the collaborative process, but it does not require every family to use that process.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a collaborative divorce?

A collaborative divorce is a structured process in which both spouses and their respective attorneys work toward a settlement outside traditional courtroom litigation. The participants sign a Collaborative Law Participation Agreement and address issues such as property, support, and parenting through guided discussions. Florida recognizes this process for family-law matters under its Collaborative Law Process Act and related rules. [Learn more about Florida's collaborative-law framework](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/the-collaborative-law-process-rules-this-is-how-we-do-it/).

### What are the possible benefits of collaborative divorce?

The process may give spouses more opportunity to shape practical terms for their family, communicate directly, and work with a multidisciplinary team. A neutral financial professional or mental-health professional may help address financial information or communication concerns, while each spouse's attorney provides legal advice. These are potential advantages, not guaranteed results.

### What are the disadvantages of collaborative divorce?

Collaborative divorce requires voluntary, good-faith participation and meaningful financial disclosure. If the spouses cannot continue working together, if safety or coercion is a concern. Or if important information remains undisclosed, the process may end and the spouses may need another legal path. Ending the collaborative process can also require new counsel for litigation, so the participation agreement should be reviewed carefully before signing.

### Is collaborative divorce more expensive than other options?

There is no universal answer. The total expense depends on the professionals involved, the number and complexity of unresolved issues, and how efficiently the spouses reach workable terms. Collaborative divorce may involve more than one type of professional, while mediation or an uncontested process may use a different structure. Compare the process and likely needs of your family rather than assuming one option always costs less.

### Who may be a good fit for collaborative divorce?

It may fit spouses who want a negotiated outcome, can participate voluntarily, and are prepared to exchange information and consider the other person's concerns. It may be inappropriate where there is coercion, a serious safety concern, hidden assets, or an inability to negotiate safely. A Florida family-law attorney can help you evaluate collaborative divorce, mediation, an uncontested case, or contested litigation for your circumstances.
